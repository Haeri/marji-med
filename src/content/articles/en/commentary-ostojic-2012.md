## The study

In 2012, Ostojic and colleagues published a study from Bad Oeynhausen in patients with chronic heart failure (NYHA class II and III), using the fifth-generation m.pulse MCP device from Cardiola AG. [The published results are listed here](/results/#pmid-22145938). Heart rate rose during treatment. The authors could not explain the cause and drew a parallel with physical exercise, where muscle activity can load the heart.

## Two possible explanations

In my opinion as the method's author, there are two apparent reasons.

**The device.** If a device does not reliably synchronise stimulation with the right phase of the cardiac cycle, afterload rises and tachycardia can follow. Pronounced muscle contractions then activate the muscle-venous pump and increase preload, which makes the signs of heart overload more marked. The comparison with exercise is logical in that case, but such a procedure is not MCP.

**The protocol.** Even with a correctly chosen K-factor, the delay from the R wave, and adequate synchronisation, excessive activation of the muscle-venous pump during strong contractions could cause tachycardia.

## What follows for practice

The study shows that in chronic heart failure, unloading the heart with MCP takes more than correct timing. The level of muscle activity must keep a delicate balance between oxygen demand and delivery, and must avoid raising preload.

In my reading, the conclusion should be that pronounced muscle contractions during MCP in patients with chronic heart failure increase cardiac load instead of reducing it, leading to transient tachycardia. For these patients, especially in the first week of treatment, I recommend only threshold or sub-threshold stimulation, from −50% to 0% of maximal tetanic tension.

A negative result is also a result to learn from, and I am grateful to the German doctors whose article made this visible.
