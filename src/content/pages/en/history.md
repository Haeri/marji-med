## Two older discoveries

Muscular counterpulsation depends on two discoveries. In the eighteenth century, Luigi Galvani showed that electricity makes muscle contract. In the early twentieth century, Willem Einthoven made it practical to record the heart's electrical activity, the signal that makes it possible to time a stimulus to each heartbeat.

## Diastolic augmentation

In animal experiments, Adrian Kantrowitz drew blood from the circulation into a balloon reservoir during systole and returned it through the femoral artery in diastole. This produced what became known as diastolic augmentation, and it started research into counterpulsation as a form of assisted circulation.

External devices using the same principle were built by a Harvard team, led by Birtwell, in the 1960s. They were complex and not widely adopted. In the 1970s, researchers in China reported simpler systems using sequential compression cuffs, which led to the external counterpulsation devices in use today.

## From surgery to skin electrodes

The direct predecessors of MCP were surgical. Dynamic cardiomyoplasty, dynamic aortomyoplasty and neoventriculoplasty all harness the patient's own skeletal muscle to support circulation. Prof. Larry V. Lapanashvili reviewed this approach in his paper "Automuscular system of assisted circulation for surgical correction of cardiac failure" (Il Cuore, 1992).

MCP reaches the same aim without surgery. In place of an external reservoir, it uses the natural vascular bed of the leg muscles, which are made to contract in time with the heartbeat, in the opposite phase to ventricular contraction. According to the inventor, it matters less which muscle group is used than that its work is correctly synchronised with the heartbeat.
